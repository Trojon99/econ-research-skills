# Economics Question Diagnostic

Use this reference to clarify and stress-test an economics research question. Apply judgment rather than a mechanical score.

## Separate the Objects

Keep these elements distinct:

- **Topic:** the broad subject area.
- **Research question:** the uncertainty whose answer could change an economic belief.
- **Economic object:** an estimand, mechanism, parameter, theoretical proposition, counterfactual, equilibrium response, or welfare object.
- **Evidence strategy:** the data, variation, measurement, proof, model moments, or institutional comparison that could answer the question.
- **Contribution:** the change in knowledge relative to the closest work.
- **Paper plan:** the later execution and exposition of the project.

Do not repair a weak research question by making the title or prose more impressive.

## Diagnose by Entry Point

### Broad topic

Identify:

- the relevant agents and their choices;
- incentives, constraints, information, beliefs, or frictions;
- the uncertain relationship or counterfactual;
- the economic consequence of learning the answer;
- at least one plausible rival explanation.

Turn the topic into a tension, not a category. Ask what reasonable economists could currently disagree about.

### Available data

Inventory units, time span, geography, variables, measurement quality, linkages, missingness, selection, and plausible variation. Ask what belief this data can update better than existing evidence. Reject questions created only because a regression is possible.

### Literature or gap

Establish what the closest papers ask, identify, model, and conclude. Classify the proposed unresolved issue as theoretical, measurement-based, identification-based, mechanism-based, boundary-condition-based, distributional, welfare-based, external-validity-based, or policy/counterfactual-based.

Do not equate an unsearched margin with a gap or a gap with an important question.

### Policy or institution

Define the rule, affected agents, timing, treatment or threshold, behavioral margins, comparison, anticipation, spillovers, equilibrium response, and welfare relevance. Separate institutional detail that provides identification from detail that provides economic content.

## Apply the Question Gate

Test the connected argument:

1. **Importance:** Would an answer change theory, measurement, policy, welfare, market design, or interpretation?
2. **Precision:** Does the question identify agents, setting, object, and uncertainty?
3. **Evidence leverage:** Could feasible evidence move beliefs rather than merely describe the setting?
4. **Identification or discipline:** Can the comparison, assumptions, proof, or model discipline be explained and audited?
5. **Rival explanations:** Can at least two plausible mechanisms or interpretations compete?
6. **Falsifiability:** Would some result weaken or kill the favored interpretation?
7. **Literature position:** Is the difference from the closest work substantive and verified?
8. **Feasibility:** Can a cheap pilot resolve the largest uncertainty?
9. **Audience:** Is there a group of economists for whom the answer matters?

## Match the Economic Design

For reduced-form work, define the treatment or exposure, outcome, unit, timing, estimand, source of variation, identifying assumptions, threats, and interpretation of a null result.

For structural work, define agents, states, choices, primitives, equilibrium concept, data moments, identification of primitives, counterfactual, and welfare object. Require a reason the counterfactual or equilibrium logic cannot be answered by reduced-form evidence alone.

For theory work, define the economic tension, primitives, mechanism, benchmark, proposition, comparative static, empirical implication, and what the model explains that an existing model does not.

For measurement work, define the latent construct, why existing measures fail, validation target, decision affected by measurement error, and whether a new measure changes an economic conclusion.

## Apply Kill Rules

Park or reformulate an idea when:

- the contribution is only a new country, dataset, variable, method, or policy episode;
- the method is clearer than the economic problem;
- no meaningful economic object can be stated;
- no evidence, variation, model moment, or proof can adjudicate the question;
- every possible result would be narrated as confirming the idea;
- the causal claim requires assumptions the design cannot defend;
- a structural model lacks a necessary counterfactual, welfare object, or equilibrium rationale;
- the closest literature already answers the same question with comparable evidence;
- the result matters only if its sign or significance is favorable;
- the likely audience would view the result as descriptive and description is not itself consequential.

Name a repair when possible. Prefer the cheapest test that could cause the user to abandon or substantially revise the idea.
