# Economics Literature Positioning

Use this reference to explain what relevant papers contribute and how the user's evolving question relates to them.

## Identify Intellectual Relationships

Classify relevant papers as:

- **Closest:** ask substantially the same question or identify the same economic object.
- **Mechanism:** study the same mechanism or a serious rival mechanism.
- **Evidence or identification:** provide the design, measurement, data, or model discipline the proposed project builds on or contests.
- **Setting or institution:** establish facts needed to understand the context.
- **Theory or structural foundation:** provide primitives, equilibrium logic, counterfactuals, or welfare criteria.
- **Adjacent:** address a related outcome or population without directly resolving the proposed question.

Do not inflate distance by classifying a close paper as merely adjacent.

## Analyze Each Paper

Extract only what is needed for the current comparison:

1. research question and economic object;
2. setting, data, and sample;
3. method, identification, theory, or model;
4. principal finding or proposition;
5. authors' stated contribution;
6. assumptions and limitations;
7. unresolved margin relevant to the user's question;
8. relationship to the proposed project.

Mark the authors' stated contribution separately from any inferred contribution. Use exact source support for important claims.

## Build Literature Clusters

Group papers by question, mechanism, evidence, identification, measurement, setting, equilibrium logic, welfare object, or policy counterfactual. Avoid paper-by-paper chronology unless chronology itself explains an intellectual development.

For each cluster, establish:

- what question the cluster studies;
- what credible knowledge it has produced;
- where findings disagree or rely on different assumptions;
- what remains unresolved;
- whether and how the user's question changes that frontier.

Distinguish a genuine unresolved issue from a margin that is merely absent from the current corpus.

## Apply the Closest-Paper Test

Compare the proposed project to each closest paper on:

- economic question and object;
- population, market, institution, and time;
- mechanism and rivals;
- data and measurement;
- identifying variation or model discipline;
- equilibrium, welfare, or policy interpretation;
- counterfactuals enabled;
- external-validity boundary.

Ask whether the proposed contribution survives after removing the words naming the country, dataset, policy episode, and method. If not, require a clearer change in what economists learn.

## Use a Relative Literature Map

When a map is useful or requested, organize it as:

| Cluster or paper | What it asks | Evidence or model | What it establishes | Remaining limit | Relation to this topic | Source tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Do not display a large map merely because enough citations exist. Keep the conversation focused on the user's live uncertainty unless a synthesis is requested.

## Phrase Positioning Carefully

Prefer formulations such as:

- "The closest papers establish X using Y; the proposed question differs by asking Z."
- "This interpretation depends on whether A or B is the relevant mechanism."
- "The uploaded materials support X, but they do not establish Y."
- "The apparent gap is currently an inference; a broader closest-paper search is needed."

Avoid "fills a gap," "first," "novel," or "unique" without defining the comparison set and verifying the claim.
