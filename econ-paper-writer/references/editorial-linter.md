# Economics Editorial Linter

Use this reference for substantial English drafting, rewriting, or manuscript audits. It turns broad economics-writing principles into an operational editing sequence. Treat it as a diagnostic framework, not a phrase blacklist or a substitute for research judgment.

## 1. Protect the paper before editing

Before changing structure or wording, identify the content that cannot disappear:

- the research question and the paper's main intellectual claim;
- any secondary contribution that materially changes how the paper should be understood;
- the empirical design, measurement contribution, model mechanism, or counterfactual object that supports the claim;
- key signs, magnitudes, units, benchmarks, uncertainty, and scope conditions;
- defined terminology, notation, citations, labels, and cross-references.

For a rewrite, compare the output with the source after editing. Restore protected content that was accidentally compressed away. Concision should remove redundancy before it removes an analytical object.

## 2. Edit function before diction

Determine what each sentence or paragraph is doing before polishing its wording. Useful functions include:

- **Question:** define the economic relationship, puzzle, or policy object.
- **Environment or institution:** establish the actors, rules, timing, market, policy, or friction needed for the argument.
- **Data and measurement:** map observed variables, samples, or constructed measures to economic concepts.
- **Variation and identification:** state the comparison, source of variation, estimand, and maintained assumption at the warranted level.
- **Model setup:** introduce agents, choices, states, timing, information, payoffs, constraints, or equilibrium objects.
- **Assumption:** state a maintained restriction and explain the economic role it plays when that role matters for interpretation.
- **Result:** report an estimate, descriptive fact, model implication, fit result, or counterfactual outcome.
- **Mechanism or intuition:** connect incentives or constraints to behavior and outcomes, or connect observed evidence to a proposed channel.
- **Literature relation or contribution:** state the precise margin on which the paper complements, contrasts with, or extends verified prior work.
- **Scope or limitation:** bound the claim by population, setting, time, design, model assumptions, or evidentiary strength.
- **Reader guidance:** explain why the next section, exercise, table, figure, or counterfactual is needed.

A sentence may perform two tightly related functions, but it should not survive merely because it sounds polished. If the main function is hidden by setup or rhetoric, reorder before line-editing.

## 3. Put information in reader order

When the supplied evidence permits it, place the information a reader needs to interpret the paper before supporting detail.

- In an abstract, do not make the reader wait for the question, design or model, and main finding.
- In an introduction, reveal the economic object, approach, and central result early enough that a skimming economist can recover the paper's claim.
- In a results section, state the estimand or plotted pattern before secondary specification detail.
- In a theory section, explain the economic environment before dense notation.
- In an empirical-strategy section, explain the comparison and source of variation before treating the estimator or equation as self-explanatory.

Do not front-load a stronger claim than the supplied material supports. Reader-first ordering is an ordering rule, not permission to overclaim.

## 4. Audit abstracts

A compact abstract will usually make the following objects recoverable, though the exact order and sentence count should adapt to the paper:

1. the question, object, or puzzle;
2. the setting, data, design, measurement contribution, or model used to answer it;
3. the main result with direction and an interpretable magnitude when supplied;
4. a central mechanism, counterfactual, or second contribution when it is genuinely part of the paper's claim;
5. the warranted interpretation or contribution.

Flag an abstract that spends substantial space on broad motivation while hiding the main result, names a method without the economic comparison behind it, or drops a central mechanism merely to meet an arbitrary word target.

## 5. Audit introductions

Check whether the introduction performs the following functions without forcing a fixed paragraph count:

- establish the economic object, puzzle, institutional change, or friction before a long literature inventory;
- state the research question and how the paper answers it;
- make the main quantitative result or theoretical insight visible early;
- explain the central mechanism, measurement problem, or model need when it is part of the contribution;
- position verified literature by substantive margins rather than by a sequence of paper summaries;
- preserve the hierarchy between the main contribution and strategically important secondary contributions;
- use a roadmap only when it tells the reader something paper-specific about the argument.

Do not force every introduction to begin with the same template. A fact, puzzle, institution, or sharply stated question can all work if they expose the paper's economic object quickly.

## 6. Audit empirical prose

For empirical sections, check the logic in this order:

1. **Comparison:** who or what is being compared, over what timing or margin?
2. **Variation:** what generates the identifying or descriptive variation?
3. **Measurement:** how do the observed variables map to the economic objects?
4. **Estimand:** what parameter or relationship does the specification recover under the stated assumptions?
5. **Estimate:** what is the sign, magnitude, unit, reference group or baseline, and uncertainty?
6. **Interpretation:** what does the estimate mean economically at the strength warranted by the design?

Treat robustness checks as responses to named threats, not as an unexplained inventory. Treat mechanism evidence as evidence for a channel only to the degree that the auxiliary outcome, timing pattern, subgroup result, or additional design bears on that channel. Plain heterogeneity is not automatically mechanism identification.

Use verbs that match the inferential object. For example, descriptive analysis can document a pattern; causal designs can estimate or identify an effect when their assumptions warrant it; models can imply or generate outcomes. Do not use a stronger verb to make a weak claim sound important.

## 7. Audit theory and structural prose

For model-based sections:

- introduce the agents, choices, timing, information, and central friction in prose before relying on notation;
- distinguish assumptions that are economically central from those that are mainly simplifying when the supplied material supports that distinction;
- after a proposition or comparative static, state the economic force behind the result rather than leaving the reader with only the sign of a derivative;
- translate parameter changes into incentives, constraints, behavior, and outcomes when that mapping is part of the model;
- keep primitives, estimated parameters, calibrated objects, endogenous outcomes, targeted moments, untargeted fit, and counterfactual objects conceptually distinct;
- state which conclusions are model implications rather than direct empirical estimates.

Do not invent an intuition, assumption role, or identification argument that the author has not supplied. If the omission is material, use the main skill's pause protocol.

## 8. Run the language linter

Flag and repair these patterns when they reduce precision:

- **Vague research action:** broad announcements of an intention to study a topic instead of stating the actual question or operation.
- **Empty contribution language:** claims of importance, novelty, or gap-filling without naming the margin added.
- **Method label without design content:** naming OLS, DiD, IV, RDD, an event study, or a structural model without explaining the relevant variation, comparison, or mapping.
- **Significance without scale:** describing an estimate as significant, large, or meaningful without the supplied magnitude, unit, baseline, or uncertainty needed to interpret it.
- **Causal or mechanism overstatement:** verbs such as prove, establish, drive, or explain when the design or exercise supports only association, suggestive evidence, or a model implication.
- **Empty passive or meta framing:** sentences whose subject is a writing act rather than the economic object, unless the writing act itself matters.
- **Translation-shaped openings:** broad contextual phrases that can be replaced by the actual institution, policy, friction, actor, or empirical object.
- **Generic roadmap language:** a section-number travelogue that gives no paper-specific information.
- **Jargon without analytical payoff:** technical or inflated terms that do not identify a method, model object, literature concept, or substantive distinction.
- **Unbounded extrapolation:** conclusions stated more broadly than the population, period, setting, design, or model assumptions permit.
- **Author-memo leakage:** draft-management comments, instructions to the author, or explanations of editing choices appearing in manuscript-facing prose.
- **Synonym cycling:** replacing a defined economic object with stylistic synonyms that blur whether the object has changed.

Do not mechanically delete every passive construction, hedge, technical term, or familiar transition. Keep them when they serve the argument.

## 9. Run the preservation check

After a substantial rewrite, ask:

- Is the paper still the same type of paper: descriptive, causal reduced-form, theory, structural, quantitative, or hybrid?
- Is the main contribution still visible at the same substantive strength?
- Did a secondary contribution, mechanism, data feature, design feature, key magnitude, or scope condition disappear?
- Did the revision accidentally strengthen a descriptive statement into a causal one or turn a model implication into an empirical fact?
- Are the comparison group, unit, sample, time period, and terminology still consistent?
- Are citations, equations, labels, and LaTeX commands intact?

If an important answer changed unintentionally, revise again before delivery.

## 10. Reject mechanical style rules

Do not impose the following without an explicit user, journal, or project requirement:

- fixed abstract word counts or introduction page counts;
- active voice in every sentence;
- artificial sentence-length variation intended to defeat AI detectors;
- blacklists of ordinary academic words;
- mandatory numbers of citations or contribution bullets;
- generic top-journal scoring rubrics;
- journal stereotypes presented as submission requirements;
- a rule that the literature review must always appear in one particular section;
- invented descriptive statistics, benchmarks, robustness checks, or citations added only to make prose look complete.

Prefer function, evidence, and reader comprehension over cosmetic regularity.
